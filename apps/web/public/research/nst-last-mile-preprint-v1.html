The Last Mile Is Institutional: Why Thailand’s Smart Cities Stall Between Monitoring and Action, and What One City Built to Cross It

Evidence from a flood and haze control tower in Nakhon Si Thammarat

Poon Thiengburanathum1,2  and  Non Arkaraprasertkul3,*

1 Faculty of Engineering, Chiang Mai University, Chiang Mai, Thailand (ปุ่น เที่ยงบูรณธรรม)

2 Research and Innovation Acceleration Agency for Competitiveness and Area Development (RCAD) (สำนักงานเร่งรัดการวิจัยและนวัตกรรมเพื่อเพิ่มความสามารถการแข่งขันและการพัฒนาพื้นที่ (องค์การมหาชน)), Bangkok, Thailand

3 Digital Economy Promotion Agency (depa), Bangkok, Thailand (นน อัครประเสริฐกุล) · * Corresponding author: non.ar@depa.or.th

Abstract

Thailand’s smart-city programs depend on institutions as well as technology. This embedded case examines four proposed gaps — coordination, procurement, data responsiveness and monitoring-to-action — through a public flood and haze decision-support prototype in Nakhon Si Thammarat. The evidence combines a catalog audit, dated telemetry snapshots, a repository and a participant operation audit. The catalog lists 216 datasets; 130 of the provincial office’s 139 records have metadata update dates on the 24th, a pattern that does not establish measurement freshness. FloodDash’s database snapshot contains 64.1 million retained reading rows and 10,414 registry records; a separate health check flags 2,230 of 10,412 records stale (21.4%). In the provincial snapshot, 21 of 51 water-level records have observations within two hours. Two audit checks on 26 October recorded a threshold exceedance with status zero, then a declared watch status 145 minutes later. They do not establish issuance or human notification time. The prototype preserves source clocks and incomplete coverage and links evidence to human verification. Official adoption, calibrated inundation prediction and improved response outcomes remain unestablished.

1 · Introduction

At 05:48 on 26 October 2026, the public feed of a water-level station run by Thailand’s Department of Water Resources (DWR) at Ban Bang Chan Si, in Nakhon Si Thammarat province, showed 4.25 m, in a reading taken 38 minutes earlier. The department’s own alert reference for that station is 4.00 m. Its early-warning feed carried status zero for the station, and no declared alert anywhere in the province. Near the river mouth, the Nang Phaya–Bang Luang gauge stood 0.73 m below its bank and was rising; the soil-moisture index read “saturated.” At the next recorded check, 08:13 — 145 minutes later — one station carried a declared “watch.”

The sensor measured. The number was published. The threshold existed. What the two checks cannot show is the short, unglamorous path from a number to a person who must decide something: they do not establish when the threshold was first crossed, when the agency declared, or whether anyone was warned through other channels. Measurement time, retrieval time, declaration status and human receipt are different quantities, and a public number and a working display do not by themselves make a warning chain. That path is what this paper is about.

Thailand has spent a decade promoting the “smart city.” The Digital Economy Promotion Agency (depa) was created by statute in 2017 (Thailand, 2017a), the national Smart City Thailand framework followed in 2018, and the Digital Government Administration and Services Act of 2019 obliged agencies to share data and serve citizens digitally (Thailand, 2019). Cities across the country have since been certified as Smart City Promotional Zones. Yet the familiar critique applies: the label is easy to award and hard to cash (Hollands, 2008; Söderström et al., 2014). The city we study says so in its own words: in Thailand, the term “has often been reduced to a tech showcase: install a few sensors, launch a flashy app, and call it transformation” (SLIC, 2025).

We ask a blunt question. Is the failure of smart city promotion and digital government in Thailand a failure of inter-agency coordination, of technological procurement, of data-driven responsiveness and of the end-to-end chain from monitoring to push — and can a working system that bypasses those failures be built and put to use now?

We proceed in three moves. First, we describe measurable features of the available evidence and the limits of institutional inference. Second, we report the artifact’s implemented capabilities and unclosed links. Third, the documentary city narrative frames hypotheses about leadership and incentives without estimating their causal effects.

2 · Background: a city and a program

2.1 A city that floods

Nakhon Si Thammarat City Municipality covers about 22.56 km² and some 105,000 residents according to the municipal case report (SLIC, 2025). It lies below the Khao Luang massif on the Gulf coast. Steep terrain, channel connectivity, drainage conditions and coastal backwater all matter. A dated HII probe reported maximum-discharge metadata of 245 m³/s at X.200 and 39.5 m³/s at X.203, a ratio of about 6.2. Two station attributes do not establish city drainage capacity. The prototype’s water-balance model excludes channel routing, backwater and the drainage network; surveyed connectivity, rating-curve provenance and hydraulic calibration are needed before inferring a bottleneck or street-level outcome.

2.2 A leader who lost first

In the municipal case report’s account, the city’s turn is inseparable from its mayor (SLIC, 2025). Kanop Ketchart, an engineer with a doctorate from the University of Colorado Boulder, ran in 2011 and lost amid reports of more than 21,000 missing ballot stubs. He spent the following years walking the city rather than campaigning. “He didn’t come to promise things,” a street vendor told the report’s interviewers. “He just sat down, asked questions, and remembered our names.” In 2021 he won, and his team took all 24 council seats; in May 2025 he was re-elected.

After the 2021 win he went to Bangkok to collect the city’s smart-city certificate from depa. A depa officer remembered what he brought: “an app mockup … It was cartoonish, almost naïve. But it was sincere. He wasn’t selling us something. He was asking how to make it real” (SLIC, 2025). That meeting began three years of co-design, co-funding and capacity building. One member of the depa team, described in the report as “a quiet, intense anthropologist-turned-bureaucrat,” is the second author. We use this account as context and as a source of hypotheses about leadership — not as verified electoral or causal evidence, and with the caution that the report was produced with the second author’s agency.

2.3 What the program produced

The city’s flagship became @NakhonCity, a civic channel inside the LINE messenger, run as a service by a Thai provider. The municipality reports large gains: call-center workload down 37%, about ฿2.3 million a year saved, and procurement lead times cut from an average of 14 months (2019) to 6.5 months (2024) by standardizing terms of reference and buying through depa’s Digital Catalog (SLIC, 2025). We note a caution that becomes a finding later. The same report gives two incompatible accounts of @NakhonCity’s volume — “over 72,000 issues” with resolution time falling from 6.2 to 2.7 days, and “over 31,000 citizen reports” with resolution time falling from 12.6 to 3.2 days, both for 2021–2024. A municipality that reports performance in good faith can still lack a single source of truth for its own numbers.

A second thread runs through the same years. In 2023 a five-person team from depa, KMITL and a Thai civic-tech firm won an Outstanding Team award at Taiwan’s Presidential Hackathon with Refill City, a scheme to make free drinking water easy to find, first imagined as a network of restaurants acting as refill points. Its first municipal pilot, a free drinking-water dispenser, opened in Nakhon Si Thammarat in early 2024 (Arkaraprasertkul, 2026a). On the last day of the award trip the team stood in Taipei’s Intelligent Operation Center, before a wall on which a whole city is laid out for the people who decide. “Standing in front of that wall,” the second author later wrote, “I understood that a digital twin is not just a technology. It is a way of governing” (Arkaraprasertkul, 2026a). The control tower studied here descends from that morning. These are accounts of design motivation, not evaluations of operational effect.

3 · Four deficits and a chain

The smart-city literature has long separated the brochure from the city that actually exists (Shelton et al., 2015). Its most useful finding for us is that smart-city outcomes depend less on technology than on institutions, people and governance (Nam & Pardo, 2011; Meijer & Rodríguez Bolívar, 2016; Albino et al., 2015). Digital-government research says the same thing from inside the state: information technology is “enacted” through existing institutional arrangements, and tends to reproduce them unless they change (Fountain, 2001; Bekkers & Homburg, 2007; Janowski, 2015). Most e-government projects in developing countries fail in whole or in part, typically at the gap between design and reality (Heeks, 2003). We organize these insights as four deficits.

Coordination. A hazard crosses agency boundaries that a budget does not. Implementation decays with every agency whose agreement it needs (Pressman & Wildavsky, 1973), and agencies collaborate only when someone invests in the craft of making them (Bardach, 1998; Ansell & Gash, 2008).

Procurement. Rules built to buy roads in a single, lowest-price tender are poorly suited to software, which must be tried, changed and kept running. Thailand’s Public Procurement and Supplies Administration Act B.E. 2560 (Thailand, 2017b) is transparent in principle and slow in practice for anything iterative; the delivery literature argues that the strategy is delivery itself (Greenway et al., 2021).

Responsiveness. Dashboards and indicators promise “real-time” governance (Kitchin, 2014; Kitchin et al., 2015), but a number becomes governance only when someone is obliged to act on it (Mattern, 2015). Publishing is not responding.

Monitoring-to-push. People-centred early warning is a chain — risk knowledge, monitoring, dissemination and response capability — and it fails at its weakest link, usually the last mile (Basher, 2006; UNISDR, 2006; WMO, 2022).

We combine the four into one model: a monitoring-to-push chain that runs sense → share → judge → push → act → learn (Figure 1). Each deficit breaks a particular link. The model is deliberately mechanical. If the chain is the unit of analysis, a smart city is not a set of projects but a set of working paths — rain to drainage, haze to shelter, complaint to crew — and each path can be tested.

4 · Research design

We use an embedded single-case design (Yin, 2018) with a design-science framing (Hevner et al., 2004). The case illustrates what a decision-support prototype can connect and what remains outside its authority. It cannot establish national causal effects, official adoption or changed response behavior. A critical-case argument motivates further inquiry rather than statistical generalization (Flyvbjerg, 2006).

Rival explanations include funding, technical skill and central–local authority; this design does not rule them out. We state in advance what would count against each deficit: a sub-daily public feed for the city’s rivers that works without the artifact, a declared alert that precedes the public threshold crossing, or a contract that buys iterative software in weeks. Table 1 groups the evidence. The documentary arc spans 2011–2026; telemetry results are dated snapshots, not a verified multi-year panel. FloodDash’s source catalog describes a 45-day raw-retention policy with hourly rollups. A database row count is therefore not four months of raw observations. The second author is a depa official and the authors built the artifact. We distinguish software tests, source availability, institutional adoption and public-safety outcomes.

5 · Findings: the four deficits, measured

5.1 Coordination: seven agency codes in one rain feed

The provincial snapshot contains 173 rain-station records carrying seven source agency codes: DWR (93), RID (25), FOP (20), HII (18), DISASTER (13), TMD (3) and EGAT (1). These metadata categories are not proof of seven independent legal operators or absent interoperability: the records already arrive in one aggregated response. The artifact combines that aggregation with DWR declarations and other sources whose clocks, formats and availability differ. The coordination question is whether the available interfaces support a reliable decision process.

The catalog lists 216 datasets from 34 publishers, with 139 records (64%) attributed to the provincial office and 20 publishers contributing one record each (D1). This describes catalog administration, not how agencies share data outside the catalog or why publication is distributed this way.

The operation audit documents source-access failures, intermittent DWR errors and translation rate limits from the deployment environment (D4). A source could be reachable through a Thai-host relay while unavailable from the cloud. These observed constraints do not establish provider motives or institutional responsibility.

5.2 Procurement: six and a half months versus weekly releases

The municipal report claims procurement lead times fell from 14 to 6.5 months (SLIC, 2025); that calculation has not been independently audited here. The prototype repository records 231 commits on 42 distinct commit dates beginning 22 June 2026, and 78 successful deployment-workflow runs by the audit cutoff. Runs are not necessarily distinct releases or proof of uninterrupted uptime. The current automatic workflow gates deployment on tests, while manual dispatch remains possible. The codebase contains 47 adapter modules and more than 1,900 unit tests (D5).

A procurement lead time and a prototype commit history have different scopes, costs, accountability and service obligations. They do not show that bypassing procurement is faster or better for an equivalent service. They illustrate different iteration mechanisms and motivate contracts that support testing, maintenance and correction alongside public accountability (Greenway et al., 2021).

5.3 Responsiveness: published is not current

At the 18 September 2026 capture, the median metadata update age was about 236 days and 95 of 216 records (44%) were older than a year (Figure 2). The catalog lists 1,995 resources: 485 downloaded successfully, 1,358 were skipped by scope, 140 failed and 12 exceeded size limits. The explicit title rule in Table 1 selects 32 hazard-related entries with 242 resources: 63 downloaded, 158 skipped, 13 failed and eight oversized. This selection includes 23 CSV-only entries, a mixed flood-risk analysis, plans, environmental and capacity records, a tide GIS service and DWR basin links. The metadata identify services as well as files; they do not support a blanket claim that none are machine-readable. We established no directly usable sub-daily river/rain endpoint among the inspected resources. Failed and skipped resources remain unclassified, so this is a limit of the audit, not proof that no live service exists. Both DWR rain and river-level entries list basin websites.

Of the provincial office’s 139 records, 130 have metadata update dates on the 24th. This clustering is consistent with batch administration but does not prove when observations were made or why metadata changed. A catalog clock should therefore be distinguished from a measurement clock.

Telemetry requires distinct denominators and clocks (D2, D3). FloodDash’s database response at 20:27 ICT on 4 November lists 10,414 registry records, 64,136,681 retained reading rows and 19,578,834 hourly rows. A separate health check at 20:23:59 assessed 10,412 records and flagged 2,230 stale (21.4%). These include non-Thai and global series; they are not a census of unique Thai physical gauges. In the provincial snapshot generated at 20:27:06 ICT, 21 of 51 water-level records (41%) and 59 of 173 rain records (34%) have observations within two hours. These are dated coverage measurements, not permanent outage verdicts. Missing or expired observations cannot establish safe conditions.

5.4 Monitoring-to-push: two checks, distinct clocks

The operation audit records checks at 05:48 and 08:13 ICT on 26 October (Figure 3, D4). At the first, STN2101 returned a measured level 0.25 m above its alert reference while declaration status was zero; the observation was described as 38 minutes old. The later check recorded one watch declaration. The 145-minute interval does not establish continuous exceedance, first crossing, issuance time or delivery time. Metadata place STN2101 in a west-coast basin; no downstream connection to the city gauge is established here.

The defensible finding distinguishes a measured threshold comparison from a returned declaration status. That threshold may not be the agency’s full issuance rule; validation or other criteria may apply. The audit does not establish negligence or a 145-minute notification delay. FloodDash’s 13,824 stored rule-based alert records are machine outputs, not confirmed public warnings, delivery receipts or response outcomes.

6 · The artifact: a control tower built around the chain

The control tower at nst.nonarkara.org is a public web application with a map, a one-screen brief for the mayor and a printable briefing for duty officers. It was designed backwards from the chain in Figure 1 and from a single test: what evidence and verification task should a duty operator find first? Figure 4 shows its structure. Its rules are more interesting than its stack, so we report them as design principles, each tied to a deficit. The interface follows the rule the second author used for a national city-comparison instrument: form should show how the instrument works (Arkaraprasertkul, 2026b).

Bypass the plumbing, not the authority (coordination). When the cloud was refused by the national water service, the artifact did not ask for an exception. It relayed the same public readings through a host inside Thailand, labeled them as relayed, and kept every reading’s age. The principle is to route around a default nobody owns while never pretending to be the source.

The current automatic release workflow runs type checks, tests, a dependency audit and a production build before deployment. Historical workflow counts do not establish that every change followed that path or that software gates substitute for public accountability.

Adapters exclude or label old observations and retain missing-coverage counts. Decision surfaces use explicit freshness windows rather than treating retention as validity. A recent retrieval does not renew the observation clock; an expired station is unknown, not zero.

Lead with the authority, then the evidence (monitoring-to-push). When the DWR declares an alert, that declaration is the first line of the answer; when a threshold is crossed without a declaration, the brief says both facts in that order and does not invent a warning. The design keeps the authority in charge of warning while removing the blind interval in which only a few people could see the data.

Surfaces offer a Thai brief for manual LINE forwarding, printing, camera inspection and village screening. The data.go.th provincial risk register contains 437 villages, not 437 city neighborhoods. Vulnerability counts are estimates derived from district totals. The study establishes implemented controls, not delivery into an official channel, authorized dispatch or completed evacuation.

The combined camera index contains 223 municipal and Longdo entries at the audit snapshot. Experimental readers produce image-based water or haze indicators; haze uses a dark-channel method (He et al., 2009) with sensor comparison. Calibration against surveyed levels and validated haze observations is incomplete. These are capabilities, not calibrated street depths or independently validated operational results.

The implemented path is evidence → briefing → human verification. Hydraulic scenarios and village priorities require domain and field validation; they do not close an official response chain. Haze-to-shelter additionally lacks a verified shelter registry. Receipt, action and outcome evaluation remain future work for all paths.

7 · Discussion: leadership and incentives

The artifact makes several institutional questions concrete without resolving their causes. The documentary narrative suggests hypotheses about leadership, incentives and ownership; the available evidence cannot rank their importance against technology or cost.

The municipal narrative emphasizes the mayor’s persistence and attention to whether services worked (SLIC, 2025). Incentive theory motivates examining operator behavior (Wilson, 1989), but this study has no independent interviews or causal design separating leadership from staffing, funding or other changes.

Isomorphic mimicry describes organizational forms without equivalent capability (Pritchett et al., 2013). The catalog audit is compatible with that concern but does not prove it: annual tables and administrative records can serve purposes other than live warning. The artifact tests a narrower proposition — combining existing public observations with honest clocks and explicit verification tasks. PDIA provides a design frame rather than a demonstrated explanation of outcomes (Andrews et al., 2013).

The second author frames visible working services as a source of credibility (Arkaraprasertkul, 2023, 2026a; Nye, 1990). A prototype can make a proposed workflow concrete for officials. Whether it changed ministry decisions or mayoral actions is not measured here.

7.1 The case against bypassing

The strongest objection is that bypassing bureaucracy bypasses accountability. Procurement rules exist because public money was stolen; agency boundaries exist because someone must be responsible for a warning. This prototype has no evidenced municipal service-level agreement or authorized warning mandate. When the host it depended on was starved of memory on 23 October, the national feed it relayed went dark for a time (D4). Street-level discretion, moved into software, can escape the controls that once governed it (Bovens & Zouridis, 2002), and the literature on “shadow IT” warns that systems built around official channels accumulate risks nobody is accountable for (Haag & Eckhardt, 2017). The GovTech agenda answers the same problem from the other side, by building the shared platforms and contracts that make such bypasses unnecessary (Dener et al., 2021). These are not quibbles; they are the reasons the system labels itself as unofficial on every screen.

7.2 Synthesis: bypass to prove, then hand over

We reconcile the two positions with a sequence rather than a compromise. Bypass is legitimate when it is temporary, transparent and aimed at handing over. It should prove a chain works, publish how, and give the institution the evidence it needs to own the chain: the agreements to share data, the contract to run the service, the official channel to receive alerts. The second author’s team calls this “innovation as a service,” with four rules — purposeful, practical, proven, people-first (Arkaraprasertkul, 2026a). The test of success is not that the prototype keeps running. It is that the city or the agency replaces it with something it owns, and that the replacement keeps the three rules that mattered most: silent is not calm, the authority speaks first, and every number carries its age.

7.3 Limitations

This is a participant case with dated snapshots, not a controlled effectiveness study. Retained row counts do not establish a continuous four-month raw archive. Catalog retrieval is incomplete, hydraulic scenarios and camera readers are uncalibrated, and the municipal narrative is secondary evidence. The build has not been fully costed, including author labor and AI tools. No independent receipt logs, duty interviews, dispatch records or public-safety outcomes establish actual mayor use or a completed warning chain. The monitoring-to-push evidence is one episode, not a distribution. Future work requires prospective source logging, crossing-to-declaration delays for every DWR station across a full season, duty-officer and mayor interviews with usage logs, camera calibration against surveyed staff gauges, and a contrast case: a certified city without these conditions.

8 · Conclusion

This case shows what a public decision-support prototype can expose: source and observation clocks, incomplete coverage, the distinction between a threshold comparison and a declaration, and tasks for human verification. Code alone cannot establish authorized warning delivery, calibrated inundation prediction, field action or institutional ownership. The four proposed deficits organize these questions; one participant case cannot settle them nationally. The contribution is an inspectable artifact and a bounded evidence record, with an agenda for testing the institutional links that remain open.

Ethics
